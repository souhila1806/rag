# The Water Cycle

The water cycle, also called the hydrologic cycle, describes the continuous movement of water on, above, and below the surface of the Earth. It has no true starting point, but is commonly described as a sequence of stages driven primarily by solar energy and gravity.

## Evaporation

Evaporation occurs when heat from the sun causes liquid water at the surface of oceans, lakes, and rivers to turn into water vapor and rise into the atmosphere. Oceans account for roughly 86% of global evaporation because they cover about 71% of Earth's surface. Evaporation is the primary way water moves from a liquid state into the atmosphere.

## Transpiration

Transpiration is the process by which plants absorb water through their roots and release it as vapor through small pores in their leaves called stomata. A single large tree can transpire hundreds of liters of water on a hot day. Evaporation and transpiration combined are often referred to as "evapotranspiration."

## Condensation

As water vapor rises into cooler layers of the atmosphere, it loses energy and condenses around tiny particles of dust, salt, or pollution to form clouds. This is the same physical process that causes water droplets to form on the outside of a cold glass on a humid day. Condensation is a critical stage because it transforms invisible vapor back into visible liquid droplets.

## Precipitation

When water droplets in clouds combine and grow heavy enough, they fall back to Earth as precipitation, in the form of rain, snow, sleet, or hail depending on atmospheric temperature. Global average annual precipitation over land is roughly 715 millimeters, though this varies enormously by region, from under 50mm in deserts to over 10,000mm in some tropical rainforests.

## Collection and Runoff

Once precipitation reaches the ground, it either infiltrates into the soil to recharge groundwater aquifers, or flows across the land surface as runoff into streams, rivers, lakes, and eventually back to the ocean. Some water is temporarily stored in ice caps and glaciers, which hold about 69% of the world's freshwater, delaying its return to the cycle for centuries or longer.

## Human Impact

Urbanization increases impervious surfaces like concrete and asphalt, which reduces infiltration and increases surface runoff, often leading to more frequent flash flooding. Deforestation reduces transpiration in a region, which can measurably decrease local rainfall over time. Climate change is also intensifying the water cycle globally, leading to more extreme droughts in some regions and heavier rainfall events in others.
